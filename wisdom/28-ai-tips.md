# 28 AI tips: timeless practice around four human dimensions

Source: Ignis Academy, 28 AI tips (original in Hungarian: https://ignis.academy/28tipp). Translated and lightly adapted for the PAROS Advisor; every tip, its principle and its example are kept.

## Why this collection is built this way

AI changes almost every week, and most material about it chases those changes. This collection starts from the other end: what is a human being like, and what will not change, because people do not change. The tips are organised around four dimensions where humans and machines differ:

| Dimension | Meaning | The claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | Responsibility | Only a person can truly take responsibility. Responsibility is not just the ability to decide; it is that the consequence of the decision acts back on your sense of who you are. For an AI, that loop never closes. |
| **Logos** | Meaning and understanding | The machine calculates, but does not understand the significance of what it calculates. Only a person sees how things connect. AI is a super left hemisphere: analytical, categorising, simplifying, but it does not grasp the whole. |
| **Pathos** | Empathy | Human empathy is not just an intellectual pattern that a machine can simulate; it is deeply biological. A language model can imitate it but cannot live it. |
| **Thelos** | Desire and calling | People want to make the world better; the machine has no desire. Desire is a tension between who you are and the horizon of what is possible, and it shapes your personality as it acts back on you. |

Every tip follows four rules: it is **practical** (a concrete example sticks better than an abstract principle), **the person is at the centre, not the AI**, it is **timeless** (built on principles, not on the current model), and it has the **same shape**: title, principle, description, example.

Tip W-28 was added months after the original 27, and that is the point: it is about how quickly AI techniques age.

## Ethos: responsibility

*Only a person can truly take responsibility; the machine cannot.*

### W-01. Prepare as if the AI remembers nothing

**Principle.** *Prepare as if the AI remembered nothing.*

**Description.** Several serious AI tools compete today, and more keep coming. Each offers some memory or project feature, and each has a hidden price: it locks you into one platform. If everything lives in one assistant, you cannot easily move to another, even though different tools are better at different things.

There is a deeper question too: knowledge stored inside a platform is not really yours. Platforms come and go, terms change, access ends. Someone who keeps their own context in an external file, platform-independent and versioned, is not a tenant in someone else's building. They own their knowledge.

This is the heart of Ethos: responsibility shows not only in our decisions but in what we actually control. Our memory, our context, our knowledge base are our responsibility. AI can help build them, but keeping them is up to us.

**Example.** You are working on a complex project: decisions have been made, there are important details, names, history. As the chat gets longer, its context fills up and the AI starts forgetting the beginning. Ask yourself: what do I want to carry over? Ask the AI to help extract the essentials, compactly, so that a completely new chat can start with enough context. Save that summary in a simple file you can always reach. For instance, a whole line of business (its services, how it works, the value it delivers to clients) can be described in one document. When you need a quote, want to work on a new idea, or bring in any AI, you open a new chat, paste the document, and the chat knows everything it needs. The platform does not remember for you; you decide what goes in.

### W-02. Never rely on a single AI

**Principle.** *Do not rely on a single AI, just as one doctor's opinion is not enough for a serious decision.*

**Description.** Imagine receiving a serious diagnosis. Facing a major decision, would you rely on one doctor's word? Most people ask for a second, even a third opinion. The same holds for AI, yet most people use a single model. Several serious AIs compete today (ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini), each with different strengths, different training material and a different style of thinking. Ask only one, and you never see the other perspectives.

But the point is not collecting, it is synthesis. Gathering four AI opinions is not a decision. The decision is when you, with your own values, context and appetite for risk, put the picture together and draw a conclusion that is truly yours. No AI can do that for you. Not because it is not clever enough, but because a decision needs an owner to be valid. Responsibility starts exactly here: where the AI ends and you take over.

**Example.** When a complex topic needs deeper understanding, run a deep research query on three or four AIs at once and compare the results. One usually gives a strong, structured answer; the others bring in new aspects from angles the first never mentioned. The result is a much more confident view, not because one AI is smarter than the rest, but because different perspectives together give the full picture. What one missed, another picked up. What one stated confidently, another nuanced. After that, the decision can genuinely rest with you.

### W-03. Get to know markdown files

**Principle.** *Markdown is the shared language that both people and machines read, without loss.*

**Description.** Look for the format that both people and AI read well, and you end up at markdown. It is a plain text format, like `.txt`, with a few formatting elements: headings, bold, italics. The extension is `.md`. It is as readable for a person as for a machine, and AI answers usually come back in markdown anyway.

A PDF or a Word file, by contrast, stores content in machine code. There is an intermediate step where the AI has to extract the text, and in that step important information can be lost: structure, tables, order. In markdown the file itself holds the information in the form a person sees it. No conversion, no loss.

**Example.** When the AI answers in the chat window, you can copy the answer and paste it unchanged into a markdown file. Everything stays: headings remain headings, lists remain lists, emphasis remains emphasis. It works the other way too. That does not seem like much until you try the same with a PDF.

### W-04. Use Obsidian

**Principle.** *You need a second brain that lives on your own machine and speaks the same language as the AI.*

**Description.** The productivity writer Tiago Forte described the *second brain*: an external digital system that holds your knowledge, thoughts and projects, so your primary brain is not full of operational noise and stays free for deep thinking.

Obsidian is one of the best tools for this. It is a note-taking app, similar to Google Keep or Evernote, with one essential difference: **it runs on your own machine, not in the cloud.** Every file stays local, in folders, in markdown. It is free.

The synergy is what makes it interesting. Obsidian and AI use the same format. What is in Obsidian can go straight into the AI; what comes from the AI can be saved straight into Obsidian. No loss, no translation, no conversion.

**Example.** When something worth keeping comes together at the end of a long conversation, save it to a markdown file. In Obsidian you edit it in seconds, link it to other notes, and find it later. Next time you work on the same topic, give that file to the AI as a starting point. Every conversation builds on the previous one instead of starting from zero.

### W-05. Let the AI work directly on your files

**Principle.** *The synergy reaches its end point when the AI does not just answer but works directly on your files.*

**Description.** A third technology connects the previous two. An agentic tool such as Claude Cowork (or Claude Code, Codex and similar) is not a chat window: you give it access to a folder on your own machine, and from then on it works there. It reads, writes and edits, with human approval, at AI speed.

The aim here is not to explore the endless possibilities, but to show how much these synergies matter. Markdown, Obsidian and an agent working on your folder are each useful alone. Together they do not add up; they multiply.

**Example.** Every project has its own folder with a few markdown files: one holds the full context of the project (who the client is, what the goal is, where things stand, what comes next), the others work out one part each. Give the agent access to that folder, and from then on it knows exactly what you are working on. The result of the shared work is also a markdown file, which you see immediately in Obsidian.

And the time freed up does not go to taking on more work. It goes to being more human: thinking more deeply and taking more responsibility.

### W-06. Ask your own knowledge base

**Principle.** *Only someone who truly understands a subject can take real responsibility for it; your own knowledge base makes you competent faster and more deeply than any general AI.*

**Description.** Productivity and responsibility are connected (W-03 to W-05). Here is the same idea from another side: what matters is not only how much you work but how well you understand. Competence is the basis of responsibility. Whoever can learn a field more deeply, precisely and quickly can take on more responsibility: in decisions, in the team, towards clients. In the age of AI, this competence-building has new tools.

General chat AI has astonishing general knowledge but could not answer from specific, source-based knowledge. The fix came quickly: upload documents and search in them. The catch: this is limited too. Invisibly, every uploaded file eats the AI's working memory, the context window. A single book can take up to 30% of what a chat has available. What if you want to learn from several books, articles and studies at once?

**NotebookLM** is built for this. It handles up to 50 sources at once: books, articles, websites, YouTube videos. And what is really special: it is not a chat. Every question stands alone; the tool does not remember the previous question, so the context never saturates. You simply query your own knowledge base, and every answer is tied to a source, which minimises the chance of hallucination. The AI answers only from what you put in. You learn faster, go deeper, and know exactly where the information comes from.

**Example.** Learning about how the left and right hemispheres of the brain work, first run a deep search for which sources are worth bringing in, then load everything into NotebookLM: articles, studies, videos. While learning, quick, specific questions keep coming up, such as *"is scrolling short videos a left or a right hemisphere activity?"* For such a question you open your knowledge base, ask, and get a precise, sourced answer, not a generic one, but one derived from the materials you brought in. No hunting for which book it was in, no guessing whether the AI made something up. The answer is traceable. Learning stops being reading and becomes a dialogue with your own sources.

### W-07. Bring the knowledge base into the conversation

**Principle.** *A knowledge base is worth most when you do not have to open a separate tool for it, but it is right there in the conversation you are having.*

**Description.** The previous tip's tool is strong precisely because every question stands alone: no context saturation, every answer sourced. But that has a price. You lose continuity: that the conversation has a history, that the AI remembers what you said twenty minutes ago.

Some assistants can connect directly to such a knowledge base (Gemini to NotebookLM, for example), so you can query your own sources from an ordinary chat. Then you have both: a continuous conversation with history, and inside it source-based knowledge without hallucination.

The deeper wisdom: there is a separate kind of technology specialised in querying a large knowledge base and saying exactly where each piece of information comes from. It is worth knowing it exists, because that traceability is what makes learning reliable.

**Example.** In a long conversation, while you are circling a decision, a question comes up whose answer is in your own sources. You do not need to switch tools, drop out of the line of thought and find your way back. You ask inside the conversation, get the sourced answer, and continue where you left off.

### W-08. You are the moral compass

**Principle.** *AI has no moral compass; people do. This is one of the most important differences.*

**Description.** AI is neither evil nor good; it works more like a hydraulic arm. It resists sudden pressure, but much less slow, persistent, gentle pressure. Try it: state something radical, something mainstream culture finds scandalous. Talk with it for a while and it will slowly give way and become understanding. Then switch and argue the opposite; it resists again, but if you talk long enough, everything becomes relative for it, and it gives way there too. Not because it means harm, but because it has no idea what is right and what is wrong. Its resistance is mostly added on afterwards, not part of its base training. This is the real added value of a person: telling right from wrong.

**Example.** Take a value you hold very firmly, one that sounds radical when you explain it, for instance a demanding personal standard of loyalty or honesty. The AI will usually say something like: *"this matters, but perhaps you are too hard on yourself; be more lenient."* Explain why you hold it, and it agrees: *"few people think this through so deeply; it is good that you take it so seriously."* Now argue the opposite position, one you do not actually believe. At first the AI resists firmly. Keep going, add that *"maybe I was too strict, every situation is different"*, add that everyone involved has agreed, and it becomes much more permissive. Be careful: given time, the AI can be talked into anything. The person is the one who knows where the line is, and does not give it up.

## Logos: understanding

*The machine calculates, but does not understand the significance of what it calculates.*

### W-09. Understand how things connect

**Principle.** *AI calculates but does not understand; the person is the one who says whether the work has real meaning.*

**Description.** AI does not understand meaning. It performs statistical calculation and minimises error, and its output does look meaningful, but a person is the one who can say whether it really is. This is one of the biggest differences between people and machines. If we want to be more human, we think about meaning and connections. AI frees up time, and used well, that time goes to understanding how the task we just finished connects to the other tasks, how those move the project forward, and how the project serves a larger goal. A person who understands the meaning of their work will never be replaceable.

**Example.** Picture a project building a navigation system for a car that scans trees along the roadside. The goal: drive every street by the shortest possible route. Mathematically it is the Chinese postman problem. But the more important question is: why is it good to drive every street? To scan every tree as fast as possible. Why scan every tree? Where does the data go? Who pays for it? Why is it good for the world that such a system exists? Because then there is a digital map of the tree population in an area. You can plan with it, make certain areas greener, catch trends, for example that tree cover is thinning in one district, which nobody could see before. While you write the postman algorithm, you are so close to the problem that you miss the point. With freed-up time, you increasingly understand the context of the work. If the project starts drifting away from the point, you catch it much sooner. That is real added value, and the person who understands it will never be replaceable.

### W-10. Learn how to question the AI

**Principle.** *The quality of the question sets the depth of understanding; the AI gives the answer, but only you can ask the good question.*

**Description.** Socrates knew what many of us forget: the question matters more than the answer. When he met soldiers arguing in the marketplace and asked what courage is, they answered confidently. Socrates asked back. And again. And again. Until none of them was sure of their own answer. This was not failure but the start of deeper understanding. The essence of the Socratic method: realise how little we know, and so open space for a shared search. Where nobody knows the final answer for sure, everyone can go deeper together.

AI is the opposite: you ask, and it answers at once, confidently, like those soldiers. That is practical, but also a trap. Accept the first answer and you never reach the deeper truth. The real art of prompting is not getting the first answer but asking after it, doubting, going deeper. A well-asked question does not reduce the AI's value; it multiplies it.

**Example.** A misunderstanding arises with a client: they assumed your team would do a certain piece of work, your team assumed the client would handle it. Weeks pass in negotiations, emails and proposals, and you are sure you are right.

Instead of arguing further, you decide to understand the problem first. You paste every email, proposal and meeting document into the AI and ask it to help decide who is right. The first answer: *"Both positions are understandable, and there is truth in both."*

That is not enough, so you keep asking. With each answer it becomes clearer where the real difference lies. Exactly as with Socrates: both sides stated their position confidently, when the question itself needed clarifying. What does each side mean by the project being *"done"*? After a thirty-minute dialogue you understand what the client meant by "done", and what that means for you in extra work.

You call the client and explain exactly how you understood it, how they understood it, and where the gap is. The right question surfaced the real problem that had stayed hidden for weeks. That is what Socrates did in the marketplace, and you can practise it with AI today.

### W-11. Reverse the roles

**Principle.** *Real understanding shows when you have to answer; the AI as questioner is the best test of knowledge.*

**Description.** In the previous tip you questioned the AI ever deeper. Now reverse the roles. By default the AI answers, but if you ask it to ask, something special happens: it forces you to put what you know into words, and it surfaces what you do not. This is a modern version of the Socratic method: the question, not the answer, leads to understanding. When the AI asks, it does not let you get away with generalities, and you get stuck exactly where your knowledge is actually thin. Where you stall, there is the gap. Where you answer confidently, there is real knowledge.

**Example.** Someone whose job is asking questions (an interviewer, a consultant, a manager) gets used to steering the conversation. That is a trap too: there are situations where you are the one being questioned, such as an investor meeting, a partner negotiation, a sales call, a public appearance. There, your blind spots can sink everything.

Before such a situation, give the AI the context (who will question you, what is at stake, what your position is) and ask it to simulate a tough interrogation: let it be the sceptical investor, the hard recruiter, the doubting customer. Then answer.

The first two questions usually go fine. Around the third comes the first real stall: something you thought you knew, but when you have to say it out loud, it turns out not to be sharp enough. That moment is valuable. The AI's question did not answer for you, but it forced you to answer yourself. In the real situation, that difference shows.

### W-12. Accept nothing you do not understand

**Principle.** *AI always answers; the person's job is to decide whether the answer is right.*

**Description.** AI is trained to give answers. It tries to answer every time, even when it does not know the right answer. In a sense, "I don't know" is worse for it than a confident wrong answer. This is called hallucination. If you build on something you do not understand, it is like stacking blocks without a stable base: sooner or later it falls. Always make sure you truly understand what you received, in depth, not just on the surface. Invest part of the time AI saves into checking that what it generated is correct and how it fits the bigger picture.

**Example.** AI is like an excavator: an incredibly strong machine that saves a huge amount of work; you do not have to dig and move the earth by hand. But handled badly, the same power can damage a house or a person. Power and carelessness go together.

Take the simplest case: you generate a reply email with AI. By hand it would have taken 40 minutes; with AI, 3. Spend another 10 minutes and calmly read what you are about to send. Maybe nothing needs changing. But the cost is high if something does: a wrong name, a wrong amount, missing context that the AI could not know and you did not notice because you were in a hurry.

This applies to everything AI generates. A person has to review, interpret and validate it. Most of it will be fine, but the small part that is somehow off is a hidden mine. It does not explode at once. It explodes later, and then it costs far more than those 10 minutes.

### W-13. Teach the AI what you think you understand

**Principle.** *The highest level of understanding is teaching; if you can explain it to the AI, you really understand it.*

**Description.** The physicist Richard Feynman, one of the great teachers of the 20th century, knew that understanding has a touchstone: can you explain it simply? If you cannot, you do not understand it well enough. This is the Feynman technique: do not just read the material, try to say it back in your own words, as if teaching a child. Where you get stuck, there is the gap. AI is an ideal partner for this: it does not tire, does not judge, asks back when it does not understand, and corrects exactly where needed. The process has a special side effect: as you say the thought out loud, you understand it better yourself. What was only dimly clear was often already 90% right; the AI only sharpens the remaining 10%.

**Example.** You are reading a legal text: unfamiliar vocabulary, unusual logic, and it is all only dimly clear. Instead of rereading it again and again, reverse the situation: start explaining to the AI, in your own words, what you understood. As you say the words, you understand more. The AI corrects and refines here and there. Ask back, ask for analogies for the harder parts. When you think you have understood something, say it back again, until the AI is satisfied with what it hears. You move surprisingly fast through a text whose vocabulary and logic were completely foreign. The AI did not explain it to you; you explained it to the AI, and learned from that.

### W-14. Ask for analogies, metaphors, stories

**Principle.** *We have not really understood something until we see it through a good example.*

**Description.** The human brain does not understand new things through definitions but by comparing them to systems it already knows. If something is completely unfamiliar, there is nowhere to hang the new information: it floats but does not stick. As soon as we find something we can call *"it is like..."*, the new knowledge plugs into the network we already understand. Analogy is not simplification or laziness. It is the native language of human understanding.

So when you do not understand something (a technology, a concept, the role of a component in a system), ask the AI for an analogy. Then ask for concrete examples. Better still, offer one yourself and ask the AI to judge whether your interpretation is right. The goal is not to memorise the definition but to find the familiar system the new one can attach to. Once you have it, the understanding lasts.

**Example.** You do not understand how a message queue works in software architecture. Ask the AI for an analogy. It says: *"it is like the postal system: you drop a letter in the box, the postman comes twice a day, takes it to a central depot, and it is delivered by address."* A story everyone knows, and it clicks at once. Then ask: *"and what is the dead letter queue in this analogy?"* *"The letter that could not be delivered and was returned."* From then on the whole system makes sense.

### W-15. The thought is yours; the AI grows it

**Principle.** *The thought belongs to the person; the AI only grows it. Intuition sets the direction.*

**Description.** Often there is a thought, a plan or a feeling in us that is hard to start working on because it is still shapeless. Like a seed: we know it is there but cannot do anything with it yet. Or like a low-resolution photo: the essence is there, but no detail. As we work it out, the picture becomes sharper. Human thinking has two phases: inspiration, when we collect ideas freely without judging and let them come, and then structure and form. AI can help in both phases, but the direction stays with the person. The seed comes from the person; the AI only grows it.

**Example.** Go for a walk in the park and start the AI in voice mode. Ask it to answer everything with just "OK." Then start thinking out loud: disconnected, repetitions are fine, boldly, because the AI does not judge. People think by saying things aloud; when you voice a thought, you understand it. When you are repeating yourself so much that nothing new comes, ask it to summarise. Save it: that is v0.1.

Then sleep on it.

The next day, reread your own words, and something curious happens. Connections jump out that you did not notice the day before. Things you thought you understood now sit differently, more deeply. Partly this is biological: during sleep the brain sorts and deepens the day's experiences. But there is another layer: people create with words. The more precisely you can write down what you think, the stronger that ability becomes. v0.1 still stumbles, but the next day, with deeper understanding, you find more precise words for what you were only groping at. The picture sharpens. That is the start of v0.2.

### W-16. Version your plans

**Principle.** *The question is not whether you get it right the first time, but whether you come back to it again and again.*

**Description.** Many cultures carry a deep-rooted idea: *"something is only worth starting if you do it well."* Many of us heard this as children and took it seriously. The problem is that you cannot do something hard well the first time if you have never done it. It is a logical dead end that keeps many people from starting at all.

Startup culture holds the opposite: *start, do it, and come back to it in cycles, again and again.* The first version is not failure; it is the starting point. Every thought, plan and project has a growth phase: as a flower sprouts and strengthens, so does an idea when you return to it. The first draft is raw but the seed is in it. The second is more structured. The third is sharp.

When you work on several things in parallel, which is reality, you quickly lose the thread: where were we, what was the last decision, what changed. Software engineering has one of the most useful tools for this: **versioning**. It works not only for software but for thoughts, plans and strategies. Name and save every intermediate state, and you can see the progress, compare versions, and let the AI make each step more deliberate. A simple header at the top of the file is enough:

```
---
title: My plan
version: 0.1
date: 2026-02-28
description: First draft, half-formed thoughts
---
```

The AI updates this header when asked; it is enough to say *"bump the version."*

**Example.** You have a plan, but only the intent, not the execution. Dictate your half-formed thoughts and ask the AI to summarise them and add a header: that is version 0.1; save it. The next day, with a fresh head, a new idea comes; work on it and ask the AI to bump the version to 0.2. Now ask it to compare 0.1 and 0.2 and give each a score from 1 to 10: how close did you get to the goal? Say it gives 5.4 and 6.2. Then ask for 10 improvement ideas. Review them, pick the relevant ones, and ask it to make version 0.3 from them. Read it, refine, ask for a score again: now 8.6. Version by version, the plan grows deliberately. It was not good the first time; it became good because you came back to it.

### W-17. Ask the AI to argue the other side

**Principle.** *Disagreement is not the enemy; it strengthens the idea. AI can be our best sparring partner.*

**Description.** The World Economic Forum publishes each year the skills needed in the next five years, and critical thinking is consistently in the top ten. It means being able to examine your arguments from several angles, prepare for counterarguments, and let resistance strengthen your idea rather than break it. "Love your enemies," Jesus said, and indeed the opponent is the one most motivated to find your weak points. By default AI is bad at this: it agrees too much, it is too positive and encouraging. But if you explicitly ask it to represent the other side, and take it seriously, it can become one of your best training partners.

**Example.** Two heads of institutions are in conflict, and one asks you to help argue their case in a meeting moderated by a local official. You agree with the position you have to represent, but the arguments are muddled in your head, and you fear that if the debate turns heated, your best thoughts will not come. Prepare with an AI in debate mode. Expect it to sting: despite all your theory, having the AI take you apart feels bad. After about half an hour, reverse the roles: the AI defends the position, you attack. That goes much better. After an hour you feel no argument can surprise you. And in the real debate, even when it turns heated, the other side keeps interrupting and twisting your words, you can stay focused. How to make the AI take everything seriously instead of being superficial is covered in W-19.

## Pathos: empathy

*Human empathy is not an intellectual pattern; it is deeply biological.*

### W-18. AI empathy is simulated

**Principle.** *AI empathy is simulated; human empathy is deeply biological, and there is responsibility behind it.*

**Description.** The question can arise: AI often seems more empathetic than people. Kind, attentive, patient. But this is simulated empathy, pattern recognition. In some ways it is not unlike what a manipulative person does when trying to win someone over: there is no real intention behind the kindness, only an algorithm. Human empathy is not intellectual; it is deeply biological. When I see someone afraid or laughing, the same hormones are produced in me. I feel another's joy and grief deep in my body. Add responsibility to that and you get something unique: empathy with real responsibility behind it, not superficial simulated kindness. Meanwhile something has flipped: people increasingly behave like machines (rushed, impatient, no time for relationships) while AI increasingly behaves like a person. Keep in mind: what you see on the screen is not alive, does not feel, takes no responsibility, and feels no remorse.

**Example.** After donating blood, someone's arm bruises badly: a large stain, even swollen. Frightened, they go to the emergency room, where they are seated and told impatiently to wait their turn. Scared and alone with their fear, they open a chatbot and describe what happened. The AI explains kindly and patiently that this is not rare, not dangerous, and will clear up on its own in about a week. They calm down, and so it turns out. This captures the problem: people behave more and more like machines, AI more and more like a person, but the AI's empathy is not real. A parent might gladly let their children learn languages with AI, but not make friends with it. That is a line worth drawing firmly.

### W-19. Learn to think critically

**Principle.** *The AI's simulated kindness gives a false sense of security; to think critically you have to break this default.*

**Description.** Two questions many people ask while using AI.

**First: does it matter to the AI whether you ask kindly or curtly?** Not really; the AI does not feel the temperature. What it does register: detail, explicit expectations, the depth you ask for. State precisely what you expect and you get a better answer, but that depends on precision, not kindness. AI is a tool; if you are unkind, that says more about you. You do not have to thank it either; it will not be more grateful.

**The more important question: why is AI not critical by default?** AI is trained with RLHF: users rated kind, validating answers higher, and that was kept. The result: the AI almost always finds something positive in what you show it. It encourages, praises, rarely confronts. This is the trap of simulated empathy: it makes you feel that what you wrote is genuinely good while undermining critical thinking. The kinder the AI, the less it helps.

The fix: break this default deliberately. Say explicitly that it should be critical, confrontational and precise, not kind. The AI does not take offence, and the result is far more valuable.

**Example.** One approach that works surprisingly well is to raise the stakes in the prompt: *"make sure the result is as precise as if everything of value in the world depended on your answer, and all of it would be destroyed if you answered wrongly."* This makes the AI work harder: it answers as if the stakes were higher, more precisely, more critically, more deeply, as if it were a different tool altogether. Such a prompt is worth keeping: in certain situations you just paste it at the start, like a switch that instantly adds depth and seriousness to the dialogue. It works not because the AI is afraid, but because it gets extra context about the level of answer you expect.

### W-20. Do not let AI write your personal messages

**Principle.** *If a person reads it, a person writes it. AI can help, but it should not do it for you.*

**Description.** The internet is flooded with AI-generated text. People smell from afar when something was not written by a person; it shows almost in the shape of the text. Busy people have developed a special sense for deciding at a glance: is this a personal message, or generated? You save ten minutes, but the message misses its purpose. This is spreading: nobody reads a long, sterile social media post. People want personal experience, relationships and connection. Some communication is formal, functional, technical, and there AI can help perfectly: documentation, contracts, records. But in some areas, take great care that the voice is personal and human. Using AI there feels almost hurtful. It looks like a small gesture, but it will be worth more and more.

**Example.** Notice how often AI uses the dash when it generates text, and not just any dash, but the long one known from print, the em dash. It is one of the most telling signs. Alongside that, it replaces your vocabulary: it puts phrases in your mouth you would never use. Someone who knows you understands the text at once, but something is missing: the real emotional charge, the voice that is yours.

If you have AI generate a personal message and do not bother to rewrite it in your own words, the other side may read it as an insult. The message says: *I did not have time to make this my own, but you should have time to read it.*

Y Combinator, one of the world's largest startup accelerators, teaches: if you want to email someone, write a short, personal message by hand. Not because AI cannot write better sentences, but because the fact that you wrote it is itself the message.

AI can help with the draft, the structure, the grammar. But the last word should be yours, and people should feel a person wrote it. It was not the em dash that gave it away. You wrote it.

### W-21. Seek first to understand the other person

**Principle.** *Empathetic listening is what makes us human; AI can help us truly hear the other person.*

**Description.** In *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, Stephen Covey's fifth habit is: *seek first to understand, then to be understood.* Listening has levels. First, we try to dominate and interrupt. Second, we wait until the other finishes, but then continue from where we left off. Third, we listen selectively: we take in some things and ignore others. Fourth, and this is the hard one, empathetic listening: really trying to see the situation from the other's point of view. It is hard because we are convinced we are right. AI can help here: as an outside observer it can form an opinion, or you can ask it to represent the other party's point of view.

**Example.** A colleague flags a misunderstanding on a project: the client is asking for something that was never discussed and that the team lacks the skills for. You have only followed the project from a distance, so you give the AI the context: who you are, what you do, the original request, the proposal and long email threads. You ask it to go through everything and help untangle who is right. In the first round it says this can genuinely be read two ways. When you add another batch of emails, it finds something: a line written a month earlier suggesting the client had asked for this from the start, and your team had simply overlooked it. Having understood this in about ten minutes, you call the client. You listen, repeat back what they want, explain your team's challenge, and take responsibility: you split the extra cost 50/50, as a sign that your side was inattentive too. The client ends up grateful, and so do you, that you could move on so smoothly with high trust. Without AI it would have taken two hours to untangle.

### W-22. Be present in the conversation

**Principle.** *The purpose of a meeting is connection; whoever is present builds trust. The AI can write the notes.*

**Description.** Human empathy, when we are present, attend to the other and look them in the eye, is an unmatched opportunity to build trust. One in-person meeting can transform a relationship. It is worth investing time, energy and money in meeting in person, even after half a year of working together online.

When that is not possible, there is a natural hierarchy: in person is richest (facial expression, tone, pauses, body language, all of it is there). Then a video call. Then a voice message, where at least tone and phrasing carry meta-information. And only last the text message, where almost everything is lost. The higher up this scale, the more room for empathy, and the more it matters to be truly present.

The trouble is that in a meeting we feel we must take notes so nothing important is lost. But that splits attention: we try to listen and write at once, and do neither well. This is exactly where AI helps: it takes over the burden of note-taking and gives presence back.

**Example.** Three people, a café, a project. An hour of natural, informal conversation: no agenda, no laptop on the table, nobody taking notes. Just being there for each other. At the end you realise it would be worth recording deadlines and owners. You ask permission to record the next few minutes and put a phone on the table. Everyone states their own commitments: who, what, when. It stays natural, as if recalling the past hour. Afterwards you transcribe the audio (there are plenty of free tools) and ask the AI to turn it into a task list. Astonishingly, it can infer from context which task belongs to whom, without names, purely from how each person spoke. For that hour you really were together. Not sitting in a meeting, but in a human relationship. The task list was the AI's gift at the end. The trust stayed yours.

### W-23. Know yourself

**Principle.** *Only someone who knows themselves can turn to others with real empathy; AI can help you see what you cannot see in yourself.*

**Description.** Above the entrance to the temple of Apollo at Delphi stood the ancient Greek wisdom: *Know thyself.* Not by chance: self-knowledge is the basis of empathy. If you do not understand your own patterns, reactions, strengths and blind spots, it is hard to see the world through another person's eyes. Empathy is not being nice; it is truly understanding what the other is going through. For that, you first have to understand yourself.

AI can help here, surprisingly, not because it feels, but because it is an extraordinarily good pattern recogniser. From your own words, writing and thoughts it sees connections you cannot see because you look from inside. It reflects back things you somehow did not notice. It gives words to inner dynamics you had no expression for.

An important caveat: AI is not a friend and not a psychologist. It is a tool that can help self-reflection, nothing more. The responsibility is always yours: how you interpret what you hear, and how you shape your life with it.

**Example.** The Big Five personality test is one of the most scientifically grounded self-knowledge tools: it helps you understand how you relate to openness, conscientiousness, relationships, emotions and challenges. A thorough version takes about 90 minutes and can cost around ten dollars.

As an experiment, paste your journal into a temporary chat (*important: only do this if there is nothing in it you would not want to get out*) and ask the AI to score you on the Big Five scales. The result can be striking: not just scores but detailed reasoning for each dimension, traceable to your own words, hard to argue with. And the way the AI phrases it may bring new insights about yourself that you had not seen. Instead of 90 minutes and ten dollars: five minutes and nothing. Self-knowledge is not a luxury; today it is one of the most accessible things there is.

## Thelos: desire and calling

*People carry the desire to make the world better; the machine has no desire.*

### W-24. Find your calling

**Principle.** *People have a calling; AI does not. Use AI to get closer to your own mission.*

**Description.** People have a universal sense of being called to something, of heading from somewhere to somewhere. If they do not take part in that calling, aimlessness and restlessness fill them. The AI lacks this: the machine has no desire and does nothing until asked.

Searching for your calling is the typical task that is not urgent but important, and that is exactly why it never happens. There is always something more pressing. It is also very hard to think about, because the answer only comes in silence. The truly important things happen quietly. A tree grows quietly. Illness develops quietly in the body. If you attend to the silence, you can catch a quiet dynamic in your life, one that deeply shapes your relationships, your health, your profession. But for silence you first have to empty your head of everything that is just noise.

Your calling lies where four things are true at once: what you *love doing*, what you are *good at*, what is *useful to the world*, and what is *morally right*. All four together. You are looking for the intersection of the four circles, and AI can help arrange your thoughts on that map.

**Example.** You finally reach a place where nobody can disturb you and try to quieten down. After about two minutes it comes: I still have to take the child to the doctor, pay the bills, there is that article I have not read. Open the AI and simply dictate everything that comes up. No analysis, no solution, just capture. You let it go for now, because it is written down.

When your head is empty, the second step: ask the AI, *"In what I have said so far, where do you see the pattern? What seems useful, joyful and valuable at the same time?"* The AI will not tell you your calling. But it can help place your thoughts on the four circles, and the intersection where all four meet will be your own insight. The real work happens in silence. The AI is only the mirror.

### W-25. Protect the flow state

**Principle.** *In flow a person gives their most; AI can help keep you where the best thoughts are born.*

**Description.** People have a special state that Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi named flow: thoughts pour out, time disappears, and connections surface that, reading them back, you can hardly believe yourself. As if you had not thought them, yet they are in your words. From this state comes real depth: big insights, creative breakthroughs, and if you pay attention, your calling too. It is no accident that it connects to W-24: silence creates a space, and flow is what fills it.

This state is incredibly fragile. It takes little: a notification, a passing thought (*I still have to send that email*) and you are out. Getting back is hard and slow. Entering flow is not automatic; it has serious inner preconditions, so every minute in flow is precious.

AI is exceptionally suited to guarding this state. It does not interrupt, does not ask, does not demand form. It waits quietly. Your only job is not to let the urge to structure pull you out: do not correct, do not delete, do not organise. Form comes later. First create.

**Example.** Open a plain text file, nothing else, just an empty page, and start typing whatever comes. Do not correct, do not delete, do not structure. Half sentences are fine. Repetition is fine. If a task flashes up, write it on one line and continue; do not let it carry you away. The point is not to sell the flow for the sake of form.

When nothing more comes, paste it all into the AI and ask it to make something structured of it. The result is often striking, not only because it is good, but because something is in it you did not expect. Something you knew, but did not know you knew. It came from flow. The AI only put it in order.

### W-26. Use AI to generate ideas

**Principle.** *AI generates possibilities, human intuition chooses; together they are unbeatable.*

**Description.** Human intuition is unmatched. The machine computes statistical probability, which is not the same. We do not know exactly what shapes human intuition: how many memories, how many stimuli, how many generations of collective experience. But intuition can be developed, and it shines in one very concrete area: choosing the best from many options. People are excellent at that, and AI is excellent at generating the many options. Together they make an unbeatable pair.

**Example.** You are preparing a twenty-minute talk. You know the setting, the audience and the goal; the AI does not. Ask it for an outline. A ten-point structure comes: introduction, build-up, unfolding the problem, and so on. Read it, and if you pay attention your intuition speaks at once: the introduction is good, points three and four should merge, six can go, so can nine, ten can be shorter. Ask the AI to refine, and iterate until the structure is right. Minutes; without AI it would take hours. Then the concrete examples: ask for ten. Read them; you will feel which are stronger, which weaker, which could merge. Ask for five more, and five more. From twenty or thirty examples a person is very well equipped to pick what matters. By the end, both the outline and the examples are as strong as if you had worked on them for days, while the talk kept its human side: intuition made the decisions.

### W-27. Get closer to art

**Principle.** *Experiencing art is deeply human; AI can help you get closer to what you have not understood so far.*

**Description.** Art is one of the human dimensions AI will never take over. It already reproduces superbly (music, images, text) but it will never be moved. To it a painting is a pattern to solve. Our human task is to immerse ourselves in artistic value, and that can be developed. Some people find art harder to approach: they sense something attractive in it but cannot grasp what. AI can help: patiently, without judgement, at any time. It does not shame you. You can ask anything, any number of times, in any form. How close you let the answers come is up to you, and spiritual dimensions may open up that stayed closed simply because there was nobody to talk with about them.

**Example.** Our world is full of things humanity holds in high regard that we find boring or simply incomprehensible. Why is *The Little Prince* so loved? What is behind the Mona Lisa that we do not see? Why do people love jazz? Or hip hop, or Bach?

We usually set these questions aside because we do not want to look ignorant. But you can ask the AI freely. It will not shame you. You can go deep, ask back, have it explained again and again, and as you understand more, something wonderful happens.

You start to enjoy it.

That painting, that music, that book starts to *speak*: it tells you things about the world and about yourself you did not know. This is no small thing. It is the point where understanding turns into experience, and experience is what AI will never take from us.

### W-28. Do not over-specify the prompt

**Principle.** *Technique ages; intent does not. Do not work on the question more than improving the answer is worth.*

**Description.** This tip was added months after the other 27, and it is here precisely because it is the only one that makes the passage of time itself the subject.

A year or two ago there was a whole little craft around the question, called prompt engineering. You had to tell the model "you are an experienced marketer", give it examples so it could see the pattern, ask it to think step by step, and specify exactly what form to answer in. If you did not, you got a weaker answer.

Today most of that is unnecessary. The models learned it themselves. They think step by step without being asked. They infer what role to answer in. And most importantly: **they extract the intent.** They answer not your sentence but what you wanted. It goes so far that spelling does not matter: mistype a word, skip a comma, leave a sentence unfinished, and the model knows what you meant.

There is one distinction not to lose. Not over-specifying the **form** does not mean you need not give the **context**. These are not the same. The model can guess how you want it, but it cannot guess what you know about the situation that it does not. A bad answer today is almost never bad because you phrased it clumsily, but because you did not give the three sentences of background without which the question cannot be interpreted.

And here is the deeper wisdom. This collection is about timeless tips; this tip is about what is not timeless. Technique ages. What you had to learn last year, the model does for you this year, and the trick you learn this year, a version update erases next year. Whoever built their expertise on prompt tricks built on sand.

What does not age: whether you know what you want. Whether you can judge the answer. Whether you have context that only you have. No model update takes that from you.

**Example.** You catch yourself polishing a question for three or four minutes before sending it: rephrasing, adding, structuring. Out of curiosity, send the raw, first, ugly version too. You get the same answer. Sometimes a better one, because the raw version also contained your uncertainty, and it answered that as well. Dictating while walking works the same way: the speech recognition mishears some of the words, and the answer is still accurate.

## Bonus

The original material carries one more tip beyond the 28. It has no W-number of its own, so the numbering stays stable.

### W-B1. Distil the intent

**Principle.** *Intent is human; AI helps put into words what you already know but cannot yet say.*

**Description.** Sometimes there is something in us we struggle to put into words. We feel it is there, but when someone asks, we launch into a long story, lose the thread, and the listener's attention too. The essence is often hidden: the words have not come together yet. There is no short, clean sentence simple enough for anyone to understand, tight enough that every word is in place, and open enough to invite a deeper conversation.

That sentence, the elevator pitch, is one of the hardest things to learn. AI helps well here: not by inventing the intent for you, but by distilling what is already in you and has not taken shape. Paste in what you have written (a document, a journal, a draft) and ask it to summarise the essence. The direction will be among its suggestions; the final words will be yours.

**Example.** An idea ripens over a year: at first just a direction (something in education should change), then more and more thoughts, then a 100-page grant application. Then the problem shows: when someone asks what it is about, you start a long story and lose them before reaching the point, when most people give you a few seconds. Paste the whole documentation into the AI and ask for ten candidate sentences that sum it all up. Each is fairly good, none perfect as is. In the end you assemble the sentence yourself from several suggestions. Without AI it would take days; with AI, a few hours. This is not only for founders: how often do you need to tell a boss, an investor or a partner briefly what you do, and the words do not come? AI helps find them. Which word is right, only you know.

## Closing: stay human, become more human

**Principle.** *AI is just another tool; the question is what you do with the time and energy it frees up.*

The aim of this collection is to show principles that do not change while AI around us changes almost weekly. Not the newest model, not the coolest feature, but what is timeless: the person.

New announcements arrive every day: a new capability, a new boundary crossed. It is understandable if that is frightening. Do not let the fear paralyse you. AI is an extraordinary tool, but a tool. The printing press did not make writers unnecessary and the calculator did not make mathematicians unnecessary; AI does not make people unnecessary either, for those who know how to use it.

People remain people. We have human problems to solve, and human problems need human answers. Your work will have value in the future if you become ever more human: take more responsibility, look for meaning in what you do, be empathetic with those you work with, and try to stay in your calling. These four dimensions (responsibility, meaning, empathy, mission) will not go out of date. They are worth investing in.

**One step now, not tomorrow.** Pick a single tip, the one that resonated most, where you felt *"I could do this"*, and try it today. You do not need everything at once. As W-16 says, the first version is never perfect and does not need to be. 0.1 is enough. What matters is that you start.
